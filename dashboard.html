About this model

A Bayesian dynamic factor model estimated by Gibbs sampling, following the New York Fed Staff Nowcast 2.0 methodology and tuned on our data. Indicators include payrolls, industrial production, retail sales, consumption, trade, prices, regional Fed surveys, jobless claims and financial conditions.

About this model

A 'bridge' regression: each monthly indicator is aggregated to a quarterly value (using only the months published so far), then a LASSO regression predicts GDP growth from them, re-fit every period. LASSO is an ordinary linear regression with one extra ingredient — an ℓ1 penalty that shrinks the coefficients toward zero and sets the least useful ones to exactly zero. So it automatically keeps just a handful of indicators and leaves a short, readable equation (a single penalty strength, chosen by cross-validation each period). We use it as a deliberately simple, transparent benchmark: the nowcast is literally a weighted sum of the selected indicators (the bars above), so every driver is explicit. In the nowcasting literature this kind of sparse linear model is the standard machine-learning baseline and is competitive with dynamic factor models when the indicator set is rich. The band is an approximate interval from the spread of the model's past errors.

About this model

A 'foundation model' for time series (Amazon Chronos-2). The DFM and the regularised regression are estimated on this dataset — they learn their parameters from US GDP and its indicators, and assume a specific structure (common factors, or a linear equation). Chronos works the other way round: it is pretrained on a vast, diverse library of time series — the same idea as a large language model, but for sequences — so it arrives already knowing many generic patterns of how series rise, fall, cycle and turn. We then apply it zero-shot, with no fitting to our data: we feed it the history of GDP growth plus the same quarterly indicators and it writes down the current quarter directly. The upside is that it can capture rich, non-linear patterns a factor model or linear regression cannot; the downside is that it carries no economic structure and cannot explain itself in those terms — so we probe it from the outside by leave-one-out ablation (how far the nowcast moves when each indicator is removed, shown above). The band is its native 10th–90th percentile forecast range.

About this model

Alpha status: BISTRO is newer and less tested than the beta models — it has no backtest record (its scoring starts at the horserace's 2024Q1 floor) and joined in July 2026, so treat its numbers as provisional. The BIS Time-series Regression Oracle — the Moirai time-series foundation model fine-tuned by the Bank for International Settlements on 4,925 macroeconomic series across 63 economies (BIS Working Paper No 1337, 2026). Where Chronos-2 is a general-purpose foundation model, BISTRO was purpose-built for macro data: it was trained on release-lagged, forward-filled series — the same shape as a real-time nowcasting information set — so we feed it exactly that: the history of GDP growth as it was known at each point in time, plus 13 monthly indicators, and it predicts the advance print directly. Zero-shot, no fitting to our data. Its probabilistic head produces genuine non-Gaussian densities (shown above as the full 500-draw distribution) rather than a symmetric band. Honesty notes: in the BIS's own univariate test BISTRO did not beat a simple AR(1) for US GDP specifically (it did for most other countries), and its training data runs through 2024, so scored quarters from 2024 may overlap what it saw in training — read its early standings accordingly.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does this dashboard show?

This dashboard presents a Dynamic Factor Model (DFM) nowcast of US real GDP growth (annualised quarterly rate). The model combines 18 monthly economic indicators with quarterly GDP data using Kalman filtering to produce an estimate that updates weekly as new data arrives.

How do I read the weekly evolution chart?

The top panel shows the nowcast point estimate (line) with confidence interval bands around it. The bottom panel shows stacked bars representing which data categories drove each week's revision to the nowcast. The diamond marker shows the net change for the week across all categories.

What does "Re-estimation" mean?

Re-estimation is not data revisions. It is the Kalman smoother backward-pass residual — the portion of the nowcast movement not explained by any specific data release or revision. When new data arrives, the model re-estimates the entire factor path, creating small secondary effects beyond the direct data impact. This residual captures those indirect effects.

How are data revisions handled?

Two views in the dashboard treat revisions differently:

  • Weekly evolution table: Each row is one newly published data point. Revisions to previously released data are pooled together into a single "Revisions & re-estimation" row per week.
  • Cumulative Impact by Series (in Data Release Impact Detail): Each series' bar includes both its initial releases and any subsequent revisions to that series. Only the Kalman smoother residual is separated into its own "Re-estimation" bar.

What do the confidence bands show?

The shaded bands on the charts show 50% and 70% prediction intervals; the header additionally quotes the 90% interval as a range (it is deliberately not shaded — it is wide enough to dominate the chart visually). All levels start from the Kalman smoother covariance matrix (the model's filtering uncertainty, widening when less data is available for the target quarter) and are then calibrated to the model's realized out-of-sample errors in the post-2020 regime, using conformal prediction on a 71-quarter real-time backtest. Without that step the raw bands were too narrow in the current regime (the 90% band covered only about 75% of outcomes since 2021); the calibrated 90% band is roughly twice as wide and is designed to cover 90%. Within-quarter weekly evolution charts apply the latest parameter estimates to earlier weeks' data (data are point-in-time; parameters are not) — this is what makes the news decomposition sum exactly; the scored backtests re-estimate per origin instead.

How are the error bands computed, and is the approach supported by the literature?

Two philosophies are used on this dashboard, both standard in the forecasting literature, and each model is labelled by which it follows.

  • Track-record calibration (conformal prediction) — used by the production DFM and the LASSO bridge. The model supplies the shape of uncertainty (the DFM's Kalman bands widen when little data is in and narrow as releases arrive); its own realized out-of-sample forecast errors supply the width. Concretely: take every past real-time error, standardise each by the uncertainty the model claimed at the time, and scale today's band so that 90% of those past standardised errors would have been inside. This assumes no particular error distribution and no correct model — only that future errors look statistically like past ones — and carries a finite-sample coverage guarantee (split-conformal prediction: Lei, G'Sell, Rinaldo, Tibshirani & Wasserman 2018, JASA; framework due to Vovk, Gammerman & Shafer 2005; online variants under regime change in Gibbs & Candès 2021). It was adopted here after a real-time audit found the DFM's theoretical 90% band covered only ~75% of outcomes post-2020; verified by leave-one-out coverage checks. Its limits are disclosed too: the guarantee is on-average (marginal), and it adapts only as new errors accumulate.
  • Model-generated (posterior/predictive) bands — used by the Tuned Bayesian DFM (posterior-predictive quantiles from full parameter draws, with stochastic volatility so widths adapt to the estimated current regime — the structural rather than patched answer to the same problem), Chronos-2 (the foundation model's native forecast quantiles), and BISTRO (quantiles of 500 seeded draws from its mixture output head). These are forward-looking — the model predicts its own uncertainty — but their honesty depends on the model, so they face the same audit: the Live Horserace tracks realised interval coverage and CRPS (a proper scoring rule; Gneiting & Raftery 2007) for every model as quarters resolve.
The unifying principle, whichever route a model takes: stated confidence must match the realised hit-rate, and where it doesn't, we either recalibrate to the record or say so on the board.

How is the backtest computed?

The backtest is pseudo real-time: for each historical quarter, the model is re-estimated using only data that was available at the time (reconstructed via FRED ALFRED vintages), with no look-ahead. The nowcast is compared against the BEA advance GDP estimate to compute RMSE, MAE, and directional accuracy.

What is the NY Fed comparison?

The NY Fed Staff Nowcast uses a similar DFM methodology. Reference metrics are from their published evaluation covering 2006 Q2 – 2024 Q4. The comparison is approximate because the two models use different series selections and factor structures.

What is BISTRO, why is it marked "alpha", and why does its record start in 2024?

BISTRO is the Bank for International Settlements' macro-specialised time-series foundation model (Moirai fine-tuned on 4,925 macroeconomic series across 63 economies; BIS Working Paper No 1337, 2026). It is marked alpha because it is even less tested than the beta models: it joined the Live Horserace in July 2026 with no retrospective backtest — its scored record begins at the horserace's 2024Q1 floor and grows forward, so treat its standings as provisional until more quarters accumulate. It is scored at the identical true-vintage origins as every other model and is not an ensemble member. Two disclosures: its fine-tuning data runs through 2024, so the earliest scored quarters may overlap its training window (the same caveat class as Chronos-2's unknowable pretraining exposure); and in the BIS's own univariate evaluation it did not beat an AR(1) benchmark for US GDP specifically, so it enters as a benchmark, not a favourite.

Why do the GDP numbers here differ slightly from BEA's published figures?

Growth rates on this dashboard are computed as 400×log-difference SAAR (the standard convention in the nowcasting literature), whereas BEA publishes compound annualised rates. The difference is <0.05pp in normal quarters and up to ~0.2pp in extreme ones (e.g. the 2021 rebound quarters). Scoring uses the same convention for every model, so no comparison is affected.
